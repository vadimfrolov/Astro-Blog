## Introduction

Why do companies still ignore accessibility?

The answer lies in a fundamental misunderstanding of what accessibility means. As the [W3C Web Accessibility Initiative emphasizes](https://www.w3.org/WAI/people/), accessibility is about ensuring digital technology is usable for all people — it's also about meeting the needs of real people in the real world, not just checking boxes on compliance checklists.

## The Chat Paradox 💬

Why have AI chats become so popular? The concept of chat is familiar to us all. Imagining that on the other side we have a friend who knows everything and can help us anytime resonates with everybody instantly. We all understand what chat is and how it works.

But here's the critical question: Are you sure that the UI of your product is easy to use for everybody?

## $6.9 billion question

The numbers tell a compelling story. [Research](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/153358/Nucleus-The%20Internet%20is%20unavailable.pdf) shows that inaccessible e-commerce retailers are losing out on $6.9 billion in annual revenues. This staggering figure represents real customers who are unable to complete purchases due to accessibility barriers.

## Common Reasons Companies Ignore Accessibility

### Unawareness of Benefits

Many businesses fail to understand that accessibility features improve the user experience for everyone, not just people with disabilities. Better contrast ratios, clearer navigation, and keyboard-friendly interfaces benefit all users across different contexts and situations.

### Misconception About Legal Requirements

Many companies underestimate the evolving legal landscape of digital accessibility. The [European Accessibility Act (EAA)](https://ec.europa.eu/social/main.jsp?catId=1202), which comes into force in June 2025, will require businesses operating in the EU to make their digital products and services accessible. This includes e-commerce, banking services, e-books, and more.

Beyond Europe, accessibility lawsuits have skyrocketed globally. In 2021 alone, over 4,000 digital accessibility lawsuits were filed in the United States—averaging more than 10 per day. The number of lawsuits has increased by approximately 15% each year since 2018. Non-compliance doesn't just risk legal penalties; it exposes companies to fines, negative publicity, and loss of public trust.

### Prioritizing Aesthetics Over Accessibility

Companies often prioritize the visual appeal of their websites over accessibility, operating under the false assumption that the two are mutually exclusive. In reality, both beautiful design and accessibility can be achieved simultaneously with proper planning and implementation.

### The Cost Excuse

The most popular excuse revolves around the belief that accessibility is too expensive. Unfortunately, this short-sighted approach harms not only potential customers but also the digital accessibility ecosystem as a whole.

Ironically, building accessibility in from the start is far cheaper than retrofitting inaccessible products later. Organizations that ignore accessibility often face costly rework, extended delivery timelines, and decreased productivity when forced to make their products compliant.

## Real Benefits of Accessibility

While the risks of ignoring accessibility are clear, the benefits of embracing it are equally compelling:

### Expanded Customer Base

With [1 in 4 adults in the United States living with a disability](https://www.cdc.gov/disability-and-health/articles-documents/disability-impacts-all-of-us-infographic.html?CDC_AAref_Val=https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/disabilityandhealth/infographic-disability-impacts-all.html), accessible design opens your products and services to a significantly larger audience. Research shows that [71% of web users with disabilities will simply leave an inaccessible website]https://www.med.unc.edu/webguide/accessibility/why-accessibility-matters/)—that's potential customers walking away from your business.

### Better Usability for Everyone

[Accessibility improvements benefit all users](https://www.w3.org/WAI/people/), not just those with disabilities. The W3C's research on diverse user perspectives demonstrates how accessibility features enhance usability across various situations:

- **Clear navigation** helps everyone find what they need faster
- **Better contrast ratios** improve readability in bright sunlight or low-light conditions
- **Keyboard-friendly interfaces** benefit users who prefer keyboard shortcuts
- **Descriptive link text** helps all users understand where links will take them
- **Alternative text for charts** makes data easier to understand and share

### Improved SEO and Machine Learning

Accessibility standards enhance content portability across devices and improve the quality of machine learning models. Well-structured, accessible content is easier for search engines to index, potentially boosting your search rankings.

## Consequences of Ignoring Accessibility

Beyond missing out on the benefits above, companies that ignore accessibility face serious consequences:

### Legal and Financial Risks

As mentioned earlier, accessibility lawsuits are becoming more common. Organizations face not only legal fees and potential fines but also the cost of emergency remediation work.

### Reduced Market Share

In today's socially conscious marketplace, companies perceived as insensitive to accessibility needs face:

- Loss of customer trust and loyalty
- Negative social media backlash
- Competitive disadvantage as rivals embrace inclusivity
- Damaged brand reputation that's difficult to rebuild

## Conclusion

The cost of ignoring accessibility extends far beyond potential lawsuits. It represents lost revenue, damaged reputation, and missed opportunities to serve a significant portion of the population.

With the European Accessibility Act coming into force in 2025 and growing awareness worldwide, accessibility is no longer optional—it's a business imperative.

Companies that recognize accessibility as both a moral obligation and a business opportunity will find themselves better positioned for long-term success.

The question isn't whether you can afford to invest in accessibility—it's whether you can afford not to. 🧐
